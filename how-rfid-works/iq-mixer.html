What it is physically

Two analog mixer ICs (or a single integrated IQ demodulator chip like the ADL5380) on the reader's PCB. Each mixer is a Gilbert cell — a differential pair of transistors that multiplies two signals together. One mixer gets the LO (local oscillator) signal directly (in-phase = I); the other gets it through a 90° hybrid coupler (quadrature = Q). After each mixer, a low-pass filter (RC or active) strips out the 2ω component.

The LO comes from the same PLL synthesizer that drives the transmitter — this is a homodyne (zero-IF) receiver. The reader transmits and receives on the same frequency simultaneously.

What it does — the math

The received signal is the reader's own CW reflected back, modulated by the tag's switching:

r(t) = A·cos(ωt + φ(t))

where φ(t) toggles between two values as the MOSFET switches. The I mixer multiplies by cos(ωt):

I = LPF{ A·cos(ωt+φ)·cos(ωt) } = (A/2)·cos(φ)

The Q mixer multiplies by sin(ωt):

Q = LPF{ A·cos(ωt+φ)·sin(ωt) } = (A/2)·sin(φ)

The 2ω terms are killed by the LPF. What remains is a baseband signal that preserves both the amplitude A and phase φ of the reflection.

Why two channels (I AND Q)?

  • A single mixer gives cos(φ) — but cos(+30°) = cos(−30°), so you can't tell the sign of the phase
  • With both I = cos(φ) and Q = sin(φ), you can compute φ = arctan2(Q, I) unambiguously over the full 360°
  • Together, I and Q form a complex baseband signal: I + jQ = (A/2)·ejφ
  • The magnitude √(I²+Q²) gives the amplitude (RSSI); the angle gives the phase
  • For phase-based sensing, the phase is the measurement — losing the sign would lose half the dynamic range

Why it matters

  • The backscatter signal is −60 to −80 dBm — a billion times weaker than the transmitted +30 dBm. The circulator provides ~20 dB isolation, but the receiver still has to dig the signal out of TX leakage.
  • The mixer converts the tiny RF modulation directly to baseband (kHz), where it can be filtered, amplified, and digitized easily
  • Homodyne architecture is simple and cheap (no separate LO frequency), which is why RFID readers are affordable
  • The tradeoff: DC offset from TX leakage must be cancelled (the circulator's 20 dB isolation isn't infinite)

Constellation diagram

If you plot I vs Q, the backscatter from one tag traces two points — one for each MOSFET state (Γ₁ and Γ₂). The data hops between these points at the FM0 rate. The vector between them is the modulated signal; their midpoint is the structural return.

For a sensing tag: a change in the tag's load impedance rotates Γ₁ on the constellation, which rotates the midpoint → the phase shifts. Γ₂ (short) stays roughly the same. The reader measures the angle of the midpoint.

Typical component values

  • Mixer: ADL5380 or similar IQ demodulator, 700–2700 MHz range
  • LO power: ~0 dBm from the synthesizer (buffered)
  • 90° coupler: Microstrip branchline or lumped LC at 915 MHz
  • LPF bandwidth: ~1 MHz (passes BLF, kills 2ω at 1.83 GHz)
  • ADC: 12–16 bit, ~2–5 MSPS, samples both I and Q
  • Noise figure: ~8–12 dB (reader NF, dominated by circulator leakage)

Signal flow

Input: 915 MHz RF signal from the circulator's port 3 (the receive port). This contains: TX leakage (~+10 dBm), environmental reflections, and the tiny backscatter from tags (~−70 dBm).

LO Input: 915 MHz CW from the PLL synthesizer, split into 0° (I path) and 90° (Q path).

Output: Two baseband analog signals, I(t) and Q(t), each containing the FM0-modulated backscatter data at kHz frequencies. These are digitized by ADCs and processed by the reader's DSP/FPGA to extract the EPC, RSSI, and phase per tag.
